**Subject:** How I would analyze a wholesale catalog

Hi {{contact.first_name}},

Last week I sat with a distributor catalog. 1,175 products. Unit cost, case
quantity, UPC, ASIN, that's it. No prices, no fees, no idea which of them you
could even list.

I want to walk you through what I actually did with it, because I think most
sellers do this in the wrong order and it costs them a weekend every time a
new price list lands.

**Step one. Throw out what you can't sell.**

Before I looked at a single margin, I ran the whole file against the
restrictions on the account. Brands you're gated in, categories that need
approval, ASINs that are hazmat or meltable in summer. There is no point
spending twenty minutes falling in love with a product you're not allowed to
list. That pass took the file from 1,175 to the ones worth a second look, and
it took about as long as it takes to read this email.

**Step two. Decide what a good product looks like before you go hunting for
one.**

This is the part people skip. If you don't write down your criteria first,
every product looks good at eleven at night. Mine for this catalog were
simple: at least 10% ROI after every fee, a buy box price that isn't a race to
the bottom, and enough sellers that the brand tolerates resellers but not so
many that I'd be the ninth offer at the same price.

**Step three. Let the software shrink the haystack.**

Here is where the numbers come in. With the buy box price, the FBA and
referral fees and a landed cost that includes shipping by weight, the scan
came back like this:

- 1,082 of the 1,175 products were profitable on paper. That's 92%.
- 901 of them cleared 10% ROI. That's 77%.
- The median product made roughly $3.33 a unit, at a median 40% ROI and a
  17% margin.

Read that median again. $3.33 a unit. Most of a good wholesale catalog is
made of small, boring, repeatable profit. That's not a disappointment, that's
the business. The catalogs that promise $40 a unit on everything are the ones
that go wrong.

The top of the list is where it gets interesting, and it's where I'd have
started buying:

- A reptile egg incubator: roughly $165 profit on a $227 sale.
- A canister aquarium filter: roughly $153 on $210.
- A 25 lb case of Hershey's Kisses: roughly $87 on $152.
- A UV clarifier for the same aquarium line: roughly $80 on $109.

I would not have found the incubator by scrolling. It's one line in a
spreadsheet with a vendor SKU and a unit cost. It only jumps out once the fees
and the buy box are next to it.

**Step four. Now use your judgment.**

This is the step software should never do for you. Of the products that
survived, I opened maybe thirty. For each one I looked at how many sellers
were on the listing, whether the price had been stable for ninety days or was
falling off a cliff, and whether the brand looked like it would still be
selling through this distributor next quarter.

The incubator is a great margin, but it's a slow seller with a handful of
offers; I'd buy a case, not a pallet. The Kisses are the opposite: thin-ish
margin, fast turns, and you need to watch the summer meltable window. Both
belong in a first order. They just belong in different quantities.

That's the whole method. Remove what you can't sell, decide your criteria,
let the tool cut the list down, then think about the thirty that are left.
In that order. If you do the thinking first and the filtering last you'll
spend Saturday on products you were never allowed to list.

## SELLER_18 · Day 42 · Repricing

**Editor note.** Track A, the repricing email. The micro version is a list of
one-liners; this is the argument behind it. I kept it to what the repricer
really does: strategies you choose, a floor price, and the Buy Box as the
target rather than the lowest price. I did not mention plan tiers or the Gold
name. If you want to say which plan includes it, add a line. CTA `repricing`
(app `/strategy`). About 700 words.

**Subject:** Winning the Buy Box isn't "set it and forget it"

Hi {{contact.first_name}},

I want to talk about the most misunderstood tool in Amazon wholesale, because
I've watched it quietly destroy margins on accounts that were doing everything
else right.

The repricer.

Here's the version most sellers run. You install one, you tell it "match the
lowest price," and you go back to sourcing. Six weeks later you notice a
product that used to make $6 a unit is making $1.40, and there are five of you
on the listing all matching each other down a cent at a time. Nobody decided
to do that. Five pieces of software decided it for you.

That's not a repricer problem. That's a "no strategy" problem. And a repricer
with no strategy is just a very fast way to give your margin to Amazon's
customers.

**Pricing is a decision, and the inputs to it keep changing.**

Competition changes. A new seller arrives with a lower cost basis, or the
seller who was undercutting everyone runs out of stock and suddenly the buy
box is $3 higher than your price.

Your inventory changes. Twenty units left with a reorder on the way is a very
different situation from two hundred units and a storage fee coming.

The market changes. Q4 demand is not July demand, and the price that wins in
one loses in the other.

Your objectives change. Sometimes you want velocity because the money is
better in the next order. Sometimes you want margin because you've got plenty
of stock and nowhere better to put the cash.

A tool that only knows "be the lowest" can't tell any of those situations
apart. It plays the same move every time.

**What I think a repricer should actually do.**

It should carry out the pricing strategy you'd run yourself if you had the
time to watch every listing every hour. That means three things, in this
order:

1. **A floor you set from your real costs.** Not a number you guessed. The
   landed cost, the fees, and the minimum margin you're willing to take on
   that product. The repricer never goes below it, whatever the competition
   does. If the floor gets hit, that's information: either the product is
   done or someone else is dumping stock, and either way you want to know,
   not to follow.

2. **A target that is the Buy Box, not the bottom.** Amazon does not always
   hand the box to the lowest price. Fulfilment, feedback, and how long
   you've been on the listing all count. The goal is to hold the box at the
   highest price that still holds it, and to step back up when the pressure
   comes off. Most sellers' repricers know how to go down. Very few are set
   up to come back up.

3. **A strategy per situation, not per account.** Fast-turning candy with
   thin margin and a meltable deadline wants aggression. A slow, high-margin
   aquarium filter with three sellers wants patience. Running both under one
   rule is how the candy stays profitable and the filter gets given away.

**How it works in Apex.**

You pick a strategy for each product or group of products, you set the
floor from the cost that's already sitting on your purchase order, and the
repricer moves your offer against the live competition inside those rules.
When it can't move without breaking the floor, it stops and tells you why,
rather than quietly breaking it. You can run it in a watch-only mode first
and read what it would have done before you let it touch a price.

I built it this way because the point isn't to reprice more. It's to reprice
the way you would, on every listing, all day, without you being there.

If you've got a repricer running right now, open it and find the floor
setting. If there isn't one, or it's the same number on every product, that's
the leak.

## BEGINNER_01 · Day 5 · The seven steps, expanded

**Editor note.** Track B's first email. The micro version is the list; this
gives each step one paragraph of what it actually involves and what to
ignore. I was careful not to promise what a free account can do beyond what I

**Subject:** So you want to sell on Amazon. Now what?

Hi {{contact.first_name}},

If you've watched more than ten YouTube videos about selling on Amazon, you
already know too much and not enough. Too many tactics, no order to put them
in.

So don't learn everything. Learn the order. There are seven steps, they go in
this sequence, and each one only makes sense once the one before it is done.

**1. Get your seller account set up.**

Professional plan, not Individual, because you'll be listing more than forty
units a month if this works. Have your ID, a bank account, a credit card and a
phone number ready; the verification can take a few days and sometimes asks
for a utility bill. This step is boring and it's the one people delay for six
months. Do it this week. Nothing below is possible without it, and nothing
below needs to be understood before you do it.

**2. Find legitimate suppliers.**

This is where beginners get stuck, so let me be blunt about what "legitimate"
means. A wholesale supplier is a brand or an authorized distributor that
sells to retailers with an invoice in your business name. Not a "wholesale"
website that anyone can buy from with a credit card. Not a liquidation lot.
If Amazon ever asks you to prove where a product came from, that invoice is
the proof, so the supplier has to be one that gives you a real one.

Finding them on your own means hundreds of emails and a lot of "we don't work
with Amazon sellers." That's the part we've done for you. There are 389 in
Apex, and the ones that say yes to new sellers are marked.

**3. Get catalogs.**

Once a supplier opens an account for you, they send a price list. Usually a
spreadsheet with a few hundred to a few thousand products, a unit cost, a
case size, and a UPC. That file is the raw material of the whole business.
Most beginners open it, feel their eyes glaze over, and close it. Don't. The
next two steps are how you read it.

**4. Analyze products.**

For each line in the catalog you need to know three things: what it sells for
on Amazon right now, what Amazon charges you to sell it, and what it costs
you to get it there. Price minus fees minus landed cost equals your profit
per unit. Do that for every line and sort by it. By hand that's a weekend per
catalog. With software it's minutes, and this is the one place I'd tell a
beginner not to try doing it manually, because the errors are expensive and
invisible.

**5. Check restrictions.**

Some brands and categories need Amazon's approval before you can list them.
A new account is restricted in more places than an old one. Check this before
you research a product, not after, or you'll waste your evenings on things
you're not allowed to sell. When you do need approval, the invoice from step
two is usually what unlocks it.

**6. Build a sensible first purchase order.**

Small. Ten to twenty products, a case or two of each, the ones with the best
combination of margin and steady sales. Write down the cost, the shipping,
the fees and the expected profit before you send the money, so in six weeks
you can compare what happened to what you expected. That comparison is where
you actually learn the business. Nobody's first PO is their best one; the
point is that it's small enough to survive being wrong.

**7. Send the inventory in.**

Ship to Amazon's warehouse (FBA), or to a prep centre that labels and sends it
in for you. There are rules about labels, box sizes and what has to be
bagged; your supplier or prep centre has done it a thousand times and will
tell you. This step feels intimidating from the outside and is mostly
following a checklist.

That's the game. Account, suppliers, catalogs, analysis, restrictions, a
small PO, ship it. Then do steps three to seven again with what you learned.

Everything else you've seen in those videos is a detail underneath one of
those seven headings, and most of the details don't matter until you're past
step six. The reason it feels unknowable is that nobody told you the order.

## BEGINNER_11 · Day 16 · The money objection

**Editor note.** Track B, OBJECTION_HANDLING. The micro version is good and
sharp; this keeps its spine ("then don't pretend you do") and adds the
argument for why the groundwork is worth doing with no capital, plus a plain
statement of what a first order actually costs. The dollar range for a first

**Subject:** "I don't have enough money to start"

Hi {{contact.first_name}},

Then don't pretend you do.

I mean that seriously. The worst thing you can do with this business is put a
first order on a credit card because someone on the internet said Amazon is
easy money and you felt behind. It isn't easy money. It's a real business
with real inventory, and inventory bought with money you don't have turns
every slow week into a panic.

So if the money isn't there yet, good. Here's what I'd do with the next few
months instead, and none of it costs anything.

**Set up the account.** Amazon's verification takes days, sometimes weeks if
they ask for documents. It's free. When the money arrives you don't want to
be waiting on a utility bill.

**Learn restrictions.** Which brands and categories a new account can list,
which need approval, what the approval asks for. This takes an evening and
it's the knowledge that stops you buying something you can't sell.

**Research suppliers.** Read through who will open an account for a new
seller and what they ask for. Some want a resale certificate, some want a
minimum first order, some want nothing but a business name. Pick five you'd
apply to. Apply to two; opening an account is free and the price list they
send you is the most useful education you'll get.

**Analyze catalogs.** Take that price list and run the numbers. Learn what
17% margin looks like next to 40% ROI, and why $3 a unit on a product that
sells every day beats $30 a unit on one that sells twice a month. Do this on
three or four catalogs and you'll know more about product economics than most
sellers with a year of orders behind them, because they never look at a
whole catalog, they chase one ASIN at a time.

**Build hypothetical purchase orders.** This is the one people skip and it's
the most valuable. Write the order you would place if you had the money.
Product, quantity, cost, shipping, fees, expected profit. Then wait a month
and look at what those products did. Did the price hold? Did a new seller
show up? Would that order have made money? You just ran a real trade with no
risk. Do that three times and your first real order won't be your first order
at all.

Here's the thing about money in this business. A sensible first PO is not
$20,000. It's a few hundred to a couple of thousand dollars, a case or two of
ten or fifteen products, small enough to survive being wrong. Most people
who "don't have enough money to start" could get there in a few months of
saving. What they actually don't have is a plan for what to do with it when
it arrives, so they either spend it badly or never spend it at all.

Do the groundwork now, while the money isn't there to be tempted with. Then
when you're ready to deploy capital, you're deploying it into an account
that's verified, suppliers who already know you, catalogs you've already
read, and an order you've already tested on paper.

That seller starts on day one with six months of experience. The one who
borrowed to start fast starts from zero, with interest.

Everything I just listed you can do from a free Apex account: the supplier
list, the restrictions, the walkthrough. Start with the suppliers; pick your
five.
